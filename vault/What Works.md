# What Works

The scoreboard for every idea tested. A ✅ needs the pre-registered bar: significant after correcting for the number of tests in **both** 2006-2015 and 2016+, positive when entering a session later, positive against surviving stocks, and positive in most years. See [[Methodology]].

## Bottom line

- On average a stock beat the S&P 500 by **+0.8%** over the 3 months after an insider purchase was filed (95% CI +0.1% to +1.7%, 99,573 signals since 2006). That is roughly +3.4% a year, but the median stock *lagged* by 0.9%.
- The edge was real in **2006-2015** (+7.8% a year, p 0.008) and has **faded since 2016** (+1.1% a year, p 0.754).
- The best simulated portfolio (*Exec/director growing stake 25%+ with $100K+*) returned +11.7% a year after costs with a 61% max drawdown, against SPY's +11.1% and 55%.
- Survivorship: about half of all insider buys are in companies that no longer trade and can't be priced. If those did like a typical priced buy, the average falls from +0.9% to -0.1%; if they did badly, to -13.5%.

## What could make this wrong

- **Survivorship (flatters results).** Only companies still listed today can be priced: coverage is 24% of buys in 2006 up to 85% in 2026. The survivor index test: +0.4% vs +0.8% against SPY.
- **The move before you can act.** Between the insider's trade and the first possible entry the stock already moved +1.3% on average. Entering one session later (variant C): +0.6%.
- **Crisis rebounds.** 30% of all excess return came from Sep 2008-Jun 2009 and Mar-Jun 2020.
- **Costs are assumed**, not measured (0.1-1% per side by price, more for illiquid names). See the 0x/2x cost runs in each strategy note.
- **Market beta only.** No size or value adjustment; small caps may explain part of any edge.
- **Placebo.** The same stocks a year earlier returned -1.0%; the insider timing adds +1.6%.
